Buying a used car in Nairobi usually means checking the engine, the logbook and the service history. Few buyers think to check the interior for pests, but a car's cabin can carry a hidden cockroach or bed bug problem straight from its previous owner into your daily life. Both pests travel well in a vehicle's tight, warm spaces, and a problem that was manageable for the seller can feel like it came from nowhere once the car is yours.

This guide covers what to look for before you buy, and what your options are if you only notice the signs after the sale is done.

## Why a used car can have a pest problem

Cockroaches and bed bugs do not appear in a car on their own. They travel in on bags, boxes, food wrappers, clothing and second-hand items left in the cabin or boot, then settle into the seams, panel gaps and foam that a car's interior offers in abundance. A car used for ride-hailing, matatu service, or by someone who regularly carries groceries or deliveries, has more of this traffic than a private car driven by one household, so it is worth being more careful when buying from these sources. A car that has sat closed up for a while, such as one kept in storage before a sale, can also let an existing problem go unnoticed until someone starts using it again.

## Signs of cockroaches to check for

- **A musty, oily smell** inside the cabin with no obvious source, especially noticeable with the windows closed.
- **Small, dark droppings** resembling ground coffee or pepper flecks in the glovebox, door pockets, under seats or in the boot.
- **Egg cases (ootheca)**, small brown capsules, tucked into seam lines, under the dashboard edge or behind trim panels.
- **Live cockroaches**, especially at night or when the car has been parked and closed up for a few hours.
- **Smear marks** along edges where cockroaches travel repeatedly, such as door sills and footwell edges.

## Signs of bed bugs to check for

- **Small, dark spotting** along seat seams, headrests or the fabric of the boot lining. This is bed bug waste, not dirt, and does not wipe off easily.
- **Shed skins**, pale and papery, caught in seam lines or crevices.
- **Bites appearing after a test drive or short journey**, particularly on the legs or arms, though not everyone reacts to bites in a way that is immediately obvious.
- **A car recently used to transport second-hand furniture, mattresses, or bags from an unknown source**, which is a common way bed bugs are introduced into a vehicle in the first place.

## A pre-purchase check you can do in a few minutes

| Area | What to check |
|---|---|
| Seat seams and headrests | Dark spotting, shed skins, or live insects when you run a finger along the seam |
| Under the seats | Droppings, egg cases, or live cockroaches disturbed when you look underneath |
| Glovebox and door pockets | Droppings or live insects, especially in a car that has been closed for a while |
| Boot and boot lining | Smell, droppings, or signs around any boxes or bags left inside |
| General smell | A musty or oily smell with the windows closed for a few minutes before you open them |

None of these checks take long, and a seller with nothing to hide should not mind you looking.

## What to do if you find signs before buying

Raise it with the seller directly. A reasonable seller will either have the car treated before the sale or adjust the price to reflect it. If you go ahead with the purchase regardless, treat the pest problem before you start using the car daily, since both cockroaches and bed bugs are easier to deal with early than once they are established through regular use.

## What to do if you find signs after you have already bought it

This is a common situation, and it does not mean you made a mistake buying the car. For bed bugs specifically, more than one treatment is usually needed, because eggs already laid are protected from the first treatment and hatch 6 to 10 days later. ## Why a shop-bought spray is usually not enough

A car's interior concentrates the same limitation a shop-bought fogger has in a room, just in a smaller space: it kills what is out in the open and does not reach what is inside seat seams, panel gaps and foam padding. For an established problem, this means the pests you do not see at the time are likely to still be there a few days later. ## Prevention once you own the car

- Vacuum the cabin, boot and under the seats regularly, which removes stray food debris that attracts cockroaches and disturbs any early activity before it is established.
- Avoid leaving second-hand bags, boxes or furniture in the car for longer than necessary, especially items whose origin you do not know.
- If you buy a used car from a ride-hailing or matatu background, consider having it checked and treated as a precaution, the same way you would check its mechanical condition.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every used car need to be fumigated before I buy it?

No. Most used cars do not have a pest problem. The checks above take a few minutes and are worth doing, but they are a precaution, not a reason to assume every used car is affected.

### Can I treat the car myself with a shop-bought spray?

You can, but for an established cockroach or bed bug problem it usually only deals with what is out in the open at the time, not what is inside seams and panel gaps. See our guide on [DIY foggers versus professional treatment](/blog/diy-fumigation-foggers-vs-professional/) for the detail.

### How long does car fumigation take, and when can I drive it again?

This depends on the treatment used and is confirmed before the job starts. The vehicle typically needs to sit closed for a set period, then be ventilated before anyone sits inside. See our [car fumigation page](/services/pest-control/car-fumigation/) for the full process.

### Will treating the car for cockroaches or bed bugs damage the upholstery?

A professional treatment uses products suited to vehicle interiors and tests on a small, hidden area first where the material is unfamiliar. Tell the technician about leather, suede or other delicate surfaces when you book.

### Is it the seller's responsibility to deal with a pest problem found before sale?

There is no blanket rule, and it usually comes down to what you agree with the seller. Raising it before you pay gives you the chance to have it treated first or reflected in the price, which is harder to negotiate once the sale is complete.
