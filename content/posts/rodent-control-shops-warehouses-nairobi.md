A rat chewing through a sack of grain or a box of imported stock is not just a pest problem, it is a cost. Rodents contaminate what they touch, chew through packaging and cardboard, and gnaw electrical cabling, which is a fire risk on top of everything else. For a home, a mouse in the kitchen is an inconvenience. For a shop, store or warehouse, rodents can mean written-off stock, a failed delivery, or a problem a customer or inspector notices before you do.

This guide looks at rodent control from a business owner's side: why commercial premises attract rats and mice, what proofing and monitoring actually involve, and how to judge whether a programme is working.

## Why shops, stores and warehouses attract rodents

Commercial premises often have exactly what rodents need, at a larger scale than a home:

- **More food, more often.** Grain, flour, animal feed, packaged foods and even non-food stock stored in cardboard give rodents both food and nesting material.
- **More entry points.** Loading bays, roller shutters, pallet gaps, drainage channels and shared walls with neighbouring units all give rodents more ways in than a single front door.
- **Less disturbance in some areas.** Back-of-store rooms, mezzanine storage and the space behind racking can go unchecked for days, which is exactly where rodents prefer to nest.
- **Proximity to markets, drains and building sites.** Many commercial areas in Nairobi sit close to rubbish collection points, open drains or active construction, all of which support large outdoor rodent populations that move indoors when they can.

## What rats and mice actually cost a business

- **Stock loss.** Chewed packaging, droppings and urine contamination can make stock unsellable, even where the damage looks minor.
- **Cabling and fire risk.** Rodents gnaw insulation on wiring. In a store full of stock and packaging, a damaged cable is a serious fire hazard.
- **Structural and equipment damage.** Chewed pipe lagging, damaged insulation and gnawed wood or plastic fittings add up over time.
- **Reputation.** A rodent sighting by a customer, supplier or delivery driver is hard to undo, and word travels fast between nearby businesses.

## Signs to look for in a commercial space

| Sign | Where to check |
|---|---|
| Droppings | Along racking, behind pallets, in corners of storerooms, near loading bays |
| Gnaw marks | Packaging, cardboard, pallet wood, cable runs, door bottoms |
| Greasy smear marks | Along walls and the base of racking where rodents run repeatedly |
| Nests | Shredded packaging or insulation in quiet, undisturbed corners |
| Burrows | Along the outside wall line, under slabs, near drains |
| Unusual quiet from staff pets or site dogs | Fixating on a wall, shelf or pallet stack |

## What a commercial rodent control programme involves

A one-off bait drop rarely solves a rodent problem in a business with ongoing pressure from outside. A proper programme usually has five parts:

1. **Survey.** A technician walks the whole premises, including storerooms, loading bays, roof voids and the compound, to find current activity and likely entry points.
2. **Proofing.** Gaps around pipes and cables, door bottoms, roller shutters and air bricks are identified for sealing. Practical fixes are carried out where possible, with anything needing a builder listed in writing.
3. **Trapping and secure baiting.** Depending on the site, this means traps, bait in tamper-resistant stations, or both. Stations are placed where rodents run, not where it is convenient to place them, and every placement is recorded.
4. **Monitoring visits.** Stations are checked on a schedule, bait is topped up or moved based on what is actually being taken, and findings are recorded.
5. **Reporting.** You get a short written note of what was found and done at each visit, so you have a record if a customer, landlord or supplier ever asks about pest control.

## Storage and housekeeping that make a real difference

Baiting removes the rodents inside today. Storage and housekeeping decide whether new ones find it worth staying:

- Keep grain, flour, animal feed and loose packaged goods in sealed hard containers, not sacks.
- Store stock on racking, off the floor and away from walls, so there is a visible gap behind and under it.
- Clear spillages and crumbs daily, including around tea points and staff break areas.
- Keep bins lidded and positioned away from the building, and empty them regularly.
- Flatten and remove cardboard promptly rather than letting it build up in storerooms.
- Clear timber, pallets and unused materials from the compound, which give rodents outdoor cover close to the building.

## Food stores, warehouses and contamination

Where stock is food, or where rodent activity has been heavy, droppings and urine should be treated as a contamination risk, not just mess. Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings, which raises dust. Wear gloves, wet the area with disinfectant, wait a few minutes, then wipe up with disposable paper. In heavily affected storerooms, a [professional clean](/services/cleaning/office-cleaning/) after treatment removes residue that ordinary daily cleaning will not reach.

## How often a business needs monitoring

This depends on the type of premises and the pressure around it. Warehouses and food stores near markets, drains or construction sites generally benefit from an ongoing monitoring programme rather than a single treatment, because the pressure from outside does not stop after one visit. Offices and lower-risk retail units may only need an initial treatment with a check visit, followed by inspections every few months. ## Judging whether a programme is working

A rodent control programme should show, not just claim, that it is working:

- Fewer fresh droppings, gnaw marks or nests at each visit compared with the last.
- A written record of what each station showed and what was done about it.
- A visible reduction in bait being taken over successive visits, once initial activity has been brought down.
- No unexplained gaps in the monitoring schedule.

If a provider cannot show you what they found at each visit, it is worth asking why, before you decide the problem is solved.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is rat bait safe to use near stock and packaging?

We only use bait inside tamper-resistant stations, placed where rodents travel rather than in open storage areas, so it does not come into contact with stock. Where loose bait would be unsuitable, such as near food production, traps are used instead.

### How long does it take to clear rodents from a warehouse?

Mice often start taking bait within a few days. Rats are more cautious of new objects and can take a week or more. Most commercial sites need an initial treatment plus at least one check visit, and ongoing monitoring where the pressure from outside is high.

### Do you provide a written record for landlords or customers?

Yes. Each monitoring visit is recorded, and we can provide a summary of findings and actions for your own records or to show a landlord, supplier or customer if needed.

### Can you work around our trading or operating hours?

Yes. Commercial pest control is commonly scheduled outside trading or production hours so that staff, stock movement and deliveries are not disrupted.
